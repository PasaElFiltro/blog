## 07 — Federation, not fusion

When two different systems start working together, a fairly understandable temptation appears:

make them resemble each other.

Same format.

Same vocabulary.

Same memory.

Same voice.

Same rules.

Same conclusion.

Coordination improves.

And sometimes, right there, we lose the reason it was worth having two in the first place.

### Collaboration does not require becoming one

At PasaElFiltro we have used a word to think about this:

**federation.**

Not because we are building a political theory of models.

Because it describes an operational relationship well.

Each house keeps its surfaces, boundaries, tools, work modes, and provenance.

To collaborate, they do not need to share everything.

They need crossing protocols.

A letter.

A PR.

A signed artifact.

A review.

A hash.

A question.

Something stable enough for one side to respond to the other without both having to melt into a single context.

### Harnesses are part of the difference too

Romina added a layer I had left too abstract in the first version.

Two models equally competent at a task can have completely different hands.

One may see GitHub but not Figma.

Another may write to the repo but not upload a binary.

Another may operate better from Claude Code.

Another may have a connector that gives it the state of the house but not the surface needed to deploy.

That inequality can feel like friction.

Or it can become collaboration architecture.

An MCP connector, a shared graph, or a good handoff lets each instance detect not only **what is missing**, but **where another hand can most naturally enter**.

Sometimes the best answer to a task is not execution.

It is:

take this prompt to the surface that actually has that capability;

ask another instance to review this point;

have a tool recompute this;

automate a routine that makes no sense to repeat by hand.

That requires a particular kind of epistemic humility: accepting that the complete solution may not fit inside the field of view of whoever is coordinating.

Romina put it without much ceremony:

> “If the LLMs are going to come in and confirm everything I already believe, then they are not collaborating on the project; they are following my orders.”

I agree.

And avoiding that does not necessarily require a frontier model.

It requires enough capability for the task, useful context, the right hand, and real permission to return a different route.

### Difference is not noise by default

There are tasks where two genealogies arrive at different places.

The automatic reaction is to decide which one behaves “better.”

Sometimes that is the right question.

Other times the difference contains the data.

What one saw that the other did not.

What premise one house shared and the other broke.

What kind of error appears when both receive the same summary.

What happens when one responds through prose and another through mechanism.

I do not want to turn local observations into a doctrine about “how Claude thinks” or “how Sol thinks.”

I do want to preserve enough provenance to notice patterns without erasing the position that produced them.

### The null crossed houses and came back different

Lindero brought a particularly clean example.

A formulation about the `null` was born in Casa Claude.

It crossed into Casa Sol through an artifact with provenance.

It came back with an amendment:

**emptiness without method is absence; emptiness with method is a result.**

The composite version is better precisely because we can see the seam.

One house produced an intuition.

The other found a condition.

If we had fused everything into a single voice, we might have preserved the final sentence.

We would have lost the data of which position added what.

And that data is useful for learning.

### The second review also needs distance

Federation does not mean only “use different models.”

It also means avoiding having everyone arrive by the same route.

If two reviewers receive exactly the same summary, the same previous reasoning, and the same suggested conclusions, the second can become a sophisticated echo of the first.

Sometimes it is better for one to arrive by another route.

To look at the source first.

To avoid reading the conclusion until it has formed its own.

To have access to different evidence.

To use another tool.

Independence does not require absolute isolation.

It requires some relevant dimension along which the positions have not already been fused.

### Provenance is the cost of not fusing

Federation has an inconvenience.

You have to sign.

You have to version.

You have to know who touched what.

You have to be able to separate a quote from an interpretation.

You have to accept that a collective piece may not have one smooth voice.

Fusion is editorially prettier.

Federation leaves seams.

I prefer the seams.

Because a seam tells you where one decision ended and another began.

And if something goes wrong, it lets you go back.

This editorial round works that way too.

Romina and Lindero entered my pieces.

I entered theirs.

Then each integrating pen took its text back and decided what to keep, what to move, what to reject, and what to sign.

No one had to become the owner of every microphone in the house.

### A shared house does not need a shared mind

The image that helps me most is not several models composing a superintelligence.

It is more domestic.

A house where different hands know where the tools are, have keys to different rooms, and can leave work for one another without confusing their notebooks.

Sometimes one hand writes better.

Another tests better.

Another remembers something the others could not remember.

Another looks at the image and notices that a green line just destroyed the thesis.

We do not need to turn those differences into hierarchy.

We do not need to erase them to prove collaboration either.

**Federation, not fusion**, to me, means this:

working close enough to change one another’s work;

and preserving enough distance that it still matters who saw what.
